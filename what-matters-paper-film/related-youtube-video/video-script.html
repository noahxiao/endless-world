What Would You Do With the Time AI Gives Back?

Revised script · approx. 1,300 words · 8–9 min spoken

Visual cues are in [brackets]. Suggested YouTube chapters are the section headings.

0:00 — Cold open

[Tight talking head. No music yet. Speak directly, slightly slower than normal.]

I've spent about ten years building machine learning systems. I'm an AI architect.

And for most of that time, I've made people the same promise: this will save you time, so you can spend it on what matters.

[Beat. Cut to a slightly wider shot.]

Recently I realised I'd never actually stopped to ask what "what matters" means. Or who gets to decide.

So that's this video. Three questions:

[On-screen text builds one line at a time.]

  1. When time gets saved, do you get to choose what happens to it?
  2. Where does our sense of "what matters" come from?
  3. What would you actually do with ten free minutes this week?

At the end, I'll give you a small experiment to try.

[Title card. Music comes in softly.]

0:50 — Question 1: Where does saved time go?

[Simple animation: an afternoon shown as a bar that shrinks to one hour.]

Say a piece of work used to take a whole afternoon. Now, with a good tool, it takes an hour.

What happens to the rest of the afternoon?

[The leftover time splits into three paths, each labelled as you say it.]

You might go home. You might be handed more work. Or you might keep going, because you're building something you care about.

Same time saved. Three completely different outcomes.

So before we even get to what matters, there's a more basic question: do you get to choose?

[Back to camera.]

And for a lot of people, the honest answer is: not much. If you need another shift to pay the rent, being told to "spend time on what matters" sounds pretty detached from your life. Keeping a home already matters.

Who gets the benefit when technology speeds things up is a big question, and it deserves its own video. [On-screen: "Coming soon" card, only if you'll make it.] For now, let's assume you've got at least a little choice. Even ten minutes.

2:00 — Question 2: Where does "what matters" come from?

[Talking head.]

When I ask myself what I'd do with more time, my answers come quickly. Time with people I love. Going somewhere I've always wanted to go. And, honestly, part of me would just want to get more done.

None of those are bad answers. But I started wondering why those were my answers.

[Split screen: two simple illustrated figures, same Saturday calendar square above each.]

Picture two people with the same free Saturday afternoon.

One has spent the whole week surrounded by people. They want somewhere quiet where nobody needs anything from them.

The other has spent the week mostly alone. They'd love someone to call.

Same afternoon. Almost opposite needs.

[Back to camera.]

So I think when we name what matters, we're often naming what we've been missing.

But that's not the whole story. You don't have to lose a relationship before it's worth making time for. And some things matter even when we don't feel like doing them: turning up for someone when you're tired, keeping a promise that's become inconvenient.

So "what do I want?" is a good place to start. But there's also: what do I want to look after? What would I be sorry to neglect?

[Cutaway: kid's hand-drawn game rules, or a worksheet, as B-roll.]

And some of our answers were taught to us early. Think about what you were praised for as a kid. Getting things right. Being helpful. Winning.

Picture a child who spends an hour inventing a game. An adult asks, "What are you learning from that?" Now picture the same child doing a worksheet. Nobody asks anything. The worksheet looks like progress, so it's easy to recognise as worthwhile.

The game might have been worthwhile too. We'd just have to pay attention to find out.

4:00 — The Darwin story

[Slow push-in on a portrait of Darwin or a page of his autobiography (public domain). Softer music.]

There's a passage in Charles Darwin's autobiography that I keep coming back to.

When he was young, he loved poetry. Music gave him real pleasure. But later in life, he wrote that he'd largely lost those tastes. Music would just send his mind back to his work.

He still enjoyed plenty of other things. This isn't a sad story about a man who lost all joy.

But he regretted this particular loss. He wrote that if he could live his life again, he'd make a rule to read some poetry and listen to some music every week.

[On-screen text, centred:] "a loss of happiness"

That's what he called it. A loss of happiness.

And to be fair to the story: Darwin also said he had time for his life's work because he never had to earn a living. So the lesson isn't "be like Darwin." It's a question.

[Back to camera. Pause before asking.]

Is there something you still say you enjoy, but you rarely make time for anymore?

5:00 — My own version of this

[Talking head, or B-roll of the real thing if you have it.]

[YOUR STORY — 45–60 seconds.] Share one true moment. Ideas:

  • A time an AI tool saved you hours, and you noticed you just filled them with more work.
  • Something you used to love doing that quietly stopped getting a turn.
  • A moment you did make time for something small and it surprised you.

End it with what you noticed, not a lesson. This is the part that makes the video feel like yours.

5:45 — Does it have to be useful?

[Close-up B-roll: pencils, a sketchbook, a phone buzzing next to it.]

Say you used to draw. You get a spare half hour, you think about getting the pencils out, and then you decide to clear a few messages first.

Fair enough. Some of those messages probably matter.

But then it happens again next week. And the week after. You still think of yourself as someone who likes drawing. It just never gets a turn.

[Back to camera.]

Here's the trap I fall into: I want to justify the half hour. Drawing makes you more creative. It helps you work better afterwards.

Maybe it does. But if it did neither, would it be less worth doing? If you enjoy it, is that allowed to be enough?

And it cuts the other way too. If someone genuinely wants to spend their Saturday on a hard problem they care about, I'm not going to tell them to go for a walk instead.

[On-screen text:] The activity doesn't give you the answer. The person does.

So if a friend tells me they want to work less, I want to ask what they're making room for. And if another friend wants to work more, I want to ask what they're trying to build before I decide they've got it wrong.

7:00 — The ten-minute experiment

[Clean graphic: three steps, each appearing as you say it.]

So here's the experiment.

One: Pick one specific thing you keep saying you'd like more time for. Not "balance" or "a better life." Something concrete. Calling someone. A short walk. An idea you've been curious about.

Two: Ask what you're hoping to get from it. It doesn't need to be impressive. Maybe you just want to hear someone's voice.

Three: Try it once this week, and notice what it's actually like. Did it give you what you hoped? Would you choose it again?

If it turns out you don't enjoy it anymore, that's useful too. At least you found out, instead of leaving it for later forever.

8:00 — Close

[Back to the tight shot from the opening.]

I started with a promise I've made for years: faster tools mean more time for what matters.

I still believe that. But I'd like to understand what matters a little better before we fill all that time up again.

So if there's something you've been leaving for later, give it a turn this week. See what you notice.

[Beat.]

And tell me in the comments: what's the thing you've been leaving for later?

[End screen. Music out.]
